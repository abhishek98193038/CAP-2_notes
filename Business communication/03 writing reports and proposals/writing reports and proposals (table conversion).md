## Improving writing skills 

Improving your writing skills for reports and proposals is essential for effective communication in professional settings:
Improving writing skills entails: the correct choice of words, sentence constructions and structuring paragraphs

### a) Correct Choice of Words:

- Vocabulary Enhancement: Regularly expand your vocabulary. Use a thesaurus to find synonyms and explore different ways to express ideas.
- Contextual Awareness: Be mindful of the context in which you're writing. Choose words that accurately convey your intended meaning.
- Avoid Jargon: Unless you are writing for a specialized audience, avoid unnecessary jargon that may confuse readers. Use clear and simple language.
- Short Words: Short words often have clear and straightforward meanings. They help to convey ideas directly without the risk of misinterpretation. EgInform for familiarize.
- Clarity: Opt for words that precisely convey your intended meaning. Avoid ambiguity and choose words that are commonly understood by your target audience.

### 2. Sentence Construction: 

- Varied Sentence Structure: Mix up sentence lengths and structures to create a more engaging and readable text. Avoid using overly complex sentences if simplicity suffices.
- Active Voice: Prefer the active voice for most sentences. It tends to be more direct and engaging. Passive voice can make sentences less clear.
- Punctuation Mastery: Understand and use punctuation correctly. Proper punctuation helps convey your intended meaning and improves the flow of your writing.
- Word order: The chef prepared a delicious meal for the guests.
		Subject (The chef) + Verb (prepared) + Object (a delicious meal)

- Placing words : 
	- <span style="color:rgb(192, 0, 0)">After dinner, the children did their homework playing in the living room.</span>
	- <span style="color:rgb(0, 176, 80)">After dinner, the children played in the living room and then did their homework.</span>

---



- Change in the meaning with the change in the placement of words:
	- <span style="color:rgb(0, 176, 80)">She almost failed the exam</span>.
	- <span style="color:rgb(192, 0, 0)">The exam almost failed her.</span>

• Writing Polite language:

Impolite Version: "The client's proposal is totally unrealistic and lacks any practical basis. It seems like they didn't put any effort into understanding the project requirements."

Polite Version: "<span style="color:rgb(0, 176, 80)">While the client's proposal demonstrates creativity, there may be some challenges in aligning it with the project's practical requirements. Further clarification and refinement may enhance its feasibility in meeting our objectives</span>."

- Avoid using words like ‘here’ and ‘there’:
	- <span style="color:rgb(192, 0, 0)">There were sixteen members who were ready to help us in the need.</span>
	- <span style="color:rgb(0, 176, 80)">Sixteen members were ready to help us in need.</span>

---



- Avoid surplus words:
	- <span style="color:rgb(192, 0, 0)">"At this point in time, we are in the process of conducting an analysis of the market conditions in order to determine potential areas for growth in the coming year.“</span>
	- <span style="color:rgb(0, 176, 80)">"Currently, we're analyzing market conditions for potential growth areas next year.“</span>

- Avoid repetition of words:
	- <span style="color:rgb(192, 0, 0)">"The company achieved success in the market. Success was a result of effective marketing strategies.“</span>
	- <span style="color:rgb(0, 176, 80)">"The company achieved success in the market, thanks to effective marketing strategies.“</span>

- Write in a direct and concise manner:
	- <span style="color:rgb(192, 0, 0)">"It is our intention to schedule a meeting at your earliest convenience to discuss the proposed changes to the project."</span>
	- <span style="color:rgb(0, 176, 80)">"We'd like to schedule a meeting soon to discuss the proposed project changes."</span>

---



- Use "you" instead of "we”: By using "you," the focus shifts to the reader or the audience, making the report more engaging and personalized. Using a singular viewpoint in reports can create a more direct and individualized tone.
	- <span style="color:rgb(192, 0, 0)">"We recommend exploring alternative solutions to address the current challenges."</span>
	- <span style="color:rgb(0, 176, 80)">"You may consider exploring alternative solutions to address the current challenges."</span>

- Use Collocations: Collocations are combinations of words that frequently occur together in a natural and idiomatic way. Using collocations in reports can enhance the clarity, fluency, and professionalism of your writing.
	- <span style="color:rgb(0, 176, 80)">The company experienced a sharp increase in profits during the third quarter.</span>

### Analyzing Data and Writing Reports and Proposals 

- Writing reports and proposals is a common and essential task in many organizations.
- Reports are documents that present information, findings, or recommendations on a specific topic or issue.
- Proposals are documents that suggest a plan of action or a solution to a problem. They seek approval or support for implementing a particular idea. Proposals are directed towards decision-makers who have the authority to approve or reject the proposed plan.
- Analyzing data in the context of an organization means examining and making sense of information collected by the organization. It involves looking for patterns, trends, and insights that can help the organization make informed decisions.
- Writing reports and proposals involves summarizing and presenting the findings from data analysis.
- Reports convey information in a clear and organized manner, while proposals suggest specific actions or changes based on the analyzed data. These documents are crucial for communication within the organization and for making informed decisions about future plans or improvements.

---

### A Timetable For Writing Report And Proposals 

- The frequency of report writing in an organizational context can vary widely depending on the nature of the organization, its activities, and its specific needs.
- The frequency of report writing can also be influenced by external factors, such as changes in the market, shifts in the regulatory environment, or the need for strategic adjustments.
- Some common scenarios where reports are typically written:


**Regular Operational Reports:**
Many organizations generate regular operational reports, <u>such as daily, weekly, or monthly updates.</u> These reports often cover key performance indicators (KPIs), project progress, financial summaries, and other operational aspects.

**Project Updates:**
Reports are often written <u>to update stakeholders on the progress of specific projects. </u>These updates may occur at milestones or on a regular schedule, depending on the project timeline.

---

**Financial Reporting:**
Organizations commonly produce financial reports on a quarterly or annual basis. <u>These reports provide insights into the financial health of the organization, including income, expenses, and profits.</u>

**Annual Reports:**
Many organizations publish <u>an annual report summarizing their achievements, financial performance, and future goals. </u>This comprehensive report is often shared with stakeholders, investors, and the public.

**Special Reports:**
Special reports may be written <u>in response to specific events, challenges, or opportunities. </u>These reports are typically more in-depth and may require extensive research and analysis.

**Compliance and Regulatory Reports:**
Certain industries and organizations are required <u> to submit reports to comply with regulations or industry standards.</u> These reports may have specific deadlines and requirements.

**Strategic Planning Reports:**
Reports are<u> often written during the strategic planning process</u> to outline the organization's goals, strategies, and action plans for the future.

#### Conducting Secondary Research 

- Secondary research, also known as desk research, is a research method that involves compiling existing data sourced from a variety of channels. This includes internal sources (e.g.in-house research) or, more commonly, external sources (such as government statistics, organizational bodies, and the internet).
- In situations where you're not involved in the data gathering process (primary research), you have to rely on existing information and data to arrive at specific research conclusions or outcomes. This approach is known as secondary research.
- Secondary research uses existing data, which also includes published results from primary research. Researchers summarise the existing research and use the results to support their research goals.
- Secondary research comes in several formats, such as published datasets, reports, and survey responses, and can also be sourced from websites, libraries, and museums.

---

#### Primary Research 

- Primary research involves creating data first-hand by directly working with interviewees, target users, or a target market. Primary research focuses on the method for carrying out research, asking questions, and collecting data using approaches such as:
- Interviews (panel, face-to-face or over the phone)
- Questionnaires or surveys
- Focus groups
- observations
- Using these methods, researchers can get in-depth, targeted responses to questions, making results more accurate and specific to their research goals. However, it does take time to do and administer.

### 3. Analyzing Data and Information for Reports 

- Analyzing data and information is a crucial step in generating insightful and meaningful reports.
- After gathering relevant data from reliable sources, the next step is to Cleanse the data. It is important to make sure the data is accurate and doesn't have mistakes. Remove any missing or weird information.
- Data Exploration (Exploratory Data Analysis - EDA):Conduct EDA to understand the distribution, patterns, and relationships within the data before diving into formal analysis.
If the data needs adjustments to make it easier to understand, like changing units or combining categories, do that.
- Statistical Analysis: Use math or statistical methods to find trends, averages, or other important numbers.
- Hypothesis Testing (if applicable):If you have specific questions, use tests to see if there are significant differences or relationships in the data.
- Interpretation of Results: Provide context and interpretation for your findings. Explain the implications of your analysis and how it relates to the objectives of the report.

---



#### Some Important Considerations to Make Effective Analysis and Interpretations:

- Report facts as found
- Dissimilar things should not be compared to draw conclusions
- Do not draw illogical cause-effect conclusions
- Choose appropriate statistical or analytical techniques based on the characteristics of your data and the questions you're addressing.
- analyze data over specific time frames to identify trends. Understand whether the analysis should focus on short-term or long-term perspectives.
- Use visualizations effectively to communicate complex information. Choose the right type of visual representation for your data.
- Do not over simplify facts
- Clearly document the methods and processes used in your analysis.



---

#### Tips for Better Analysis of Data for Reports 

- Tailor your analysis to the audience.
- Consider the level of expertise and the specific information your audience needs.
- Maintain a judicial attitude.
- Consult others who have better knowledge on the topic you are dealing with.
- Ensure that your data is accurate, relevant, and of high quality. Invest time in thorough data cleaning.
- Choose the right tools and software for your analysis.
- Test your interpretations. Review and refine your analysis as you progress, and be open to adjusting your approach based on interim findings.
- Apply statistical methods appropriately. Ensure that your analysis is statistically sound and that you're using the right tests for your data.
- Aim for simplicity in your analysis and presentation. Avoid unnecessary jargon and complex methodologies unless essential.

### Business Reports Types 

Business reports can be broadly categorized into two main types:
1) informative reports
2) investigative reports.
3) Each type serves a distinct purpose and is structured differently.

---

1) <u>Informative Reports:</u> The primary goal of informative reports is to present facts, data, or information without any analysis or interpretation. These reports are designed to inform and educate the reader.
- Example: Progress Reports, Operational Reports, Financial Reports, Situational Reports


<u>Basic Components:</u> 
$>$ Introduction: Sets the context for the report by introducing the topic, explaining its significance, and outlining the purpose of the report.
$>$ Background: Offers relevant background information or context related to the subject matter. This section helps readers understand the history or context of the report.
$>$ Objectives: Clearly defines the goals and objectives of the report.
$>$ Scope: Defines the boundaries of the report.
$>$ Methodology (if applicable): Describes the methods used to collect data, conduct research, or perform analysis. This section adds transparency to the report's process.
$>$ Findings/Details/Feasibility: Presents the main body of the report, providing detailed information, analysis, and evidence related to the objectives. This section is the core of the report
$>$ Conclusion or Summary: Summarizes key points without providing analysis.

---

2) Investigative Reports: Investigative reports are more in-depth and involve analysis, interpretation, and often recommendations. They are used to explore a specific issue, problem, or opportunity.
- Problem-Solving Reports, Feasibility Studies, Market Research Reports etc
<u>Basic Components:</u> 
Introduction: Outlines the purpose, background, and objectives of the investigation.
	Statement of Problem
		 Objective: Clearly defines the goals and objectives of the report
		Methodology: Describes the methods used to gather and analyze data.
		Findings: Presents the results of the investigation, often supported by data and analysis.
		Conclusion: Summarizes key findings and may suggest recommendations.
		Recommendations: Proposes actions or strategies based on the analysis.
* Report writing can take various styles, and the choice of style depends on the purpose of the report, the intended audience, and the nature of the information being conveyed.
* A well-crafted report may incorporate elements from multiple styles based on the specific requirements of the task at hand.

---

### Styles of Report Writing 

- Narrative or Prose Style: This is the more common and traditional style of report writing. It involves presenting information in a flowing, paragraphbased narrative. It is like telling a story, with a logical flow from introduction to conclusion.
- Tabular or Schematic Style: This style involves presenting information in a structured, tabular, or schematic format. It relies on tables, charts, graphs, bullet points, and other visual elements to convey information. This style is often used for technical and scientific reports.
- Letter Style: The report is formatted like a formal letter, including elements such as the date, salutation, body of the letter (containing the main content of the report), and a closing. This style is commonly used for shorter reports.
- Visual Style: Emphasizes the use of visuals such as images, infographics, and multimedia elements to convey information. This style is effective for enhancing the visual appeal and understanding of the report.

### Proposal 

- A proposal is a written document that outlines a plan, idea, project, or a formal request for a specific action or decision. It is typically prepared and presented to persuade an individual, group, organization, or government body to support, fund, or approve a particular course of action. Proposals are commonly used in various contexts, including business, academia, research, and government.
- Two major types of proposals. Each serve distinct purposes and contexts.
	1) Business proposals
	2) Research proposals,

Proposals can be created by individuals, group of individuals, organizations, or teams across various sectors and industries.
The length of a proposal ranges from writing a simple e-mail to several pages document.
Terms of Reference(TOR) is a document that outlines the objectives, scope, and key details of a project or initiative. It serves as a guide for the individuals or entities involved in the project and helps set expectations, define responsibilities, and establish the framework for the work to be performed.

1. Business Proposals: Business proposals are documents presented by businesses to potential clients, partners, or investors to persuade them to engage in a specific business arrangement. The goal is often to win a contract, secure funding, or initiate a business relationship.
	<u>Components of Business Proposals:</u>
	- Introduction to the business or project: Sets the stage for the proposal, introducing the reader to the purpose, scope, and context of the document.
	- Background or Context: Provides relevant information about the business, its history, and the factors leading to the proposal.
	- Problem or Opportunity Statement: Clearly defines the problem the proposal aims to address or highlights the opportunity being pursued.
	- Objectives: Outlines the specific goals and outcomes the proposal aims to achieve.
	- Proposed Solutions or Services/ Benefits or advantages: Describes the products, services, or solutions being offered to address the problem or capitalize on the opportunity.
	- Terms and Conditions: Outlines any terms and conditions associated with the proposal, including payment terms, contract duration, and other relevant details.
	- Conclusion


2. Research Proposals: Research proposals are documents that outline the plan and methodology for conducting a research project. They are submitted to institutions, funding agencies, or research committees to seek approval and support for the proposed research.
- Variations may occur in the structure of a research proposal based on institutional or funding agency requirements:

 Abstract
 Introduction
 Aims and Objectives
 Background Significance
 Literature Review
 Research Design and Methodology
 Research Questions
 Suppositions and Implications
 Conclusion
 Bibliography

---

The most significant factor in planning a proposal is whether the recipient has asked you to submit a proposal.

i) **Solicited proposals** are generally prepared at the request of external parties that require a product or a service, but they may also be requested by such internal sources as management or the board of directors. When organizations require complex products, services, or systems, they often prepare a formal invitation to bid on the contract, called a request for proposal (RFP)

ii) **Unsolicited proposals** are created by organizations attempting to obtain business or funding without a specific invitation from a potential client. Such proposals may also be initiated by employees or managers who want to convince company insiders to adopt a program, policy, or idea.

### Revising Your Reports and Proposals 

- The revision process is essentially the same for reports and proposals as for any business message, although it may take longer.
- Evaluate your organization, style, and tone.
- Improve readability; keep paragraphs short; use lists and bullets and headings and subheadings.
- Edit for clarity and conciseness.

---

#### Presentation 

- Paragraphs should be short and concise. They should be spaced apart.
- Avoid rambling by using short sentences.
- Avoid using unnecessary jargon and unexplained abbreviations.
- List your standard measurements, units and technical terminology in a glossary at the end.
- Headings should be clear - highlighted in bold or underlined.
- Label graphs, pictures or drawings as 'Figures', e.g. Figure 1, Figure 2 etc.
- Tables of information should be labelled separately, e.g. Table 1, Table 2 etc.

---

### Components of a Formal Report 

- The components of a formal report depend upon its purpose, length, expectations of the audience, and what your organization dictates.
- Most formal reports have three main divisions:

1. Prefatory parts,
2. Text parts,
3. Supplementary parts.

- Most of the component parts of a formal report start on a new page. Some reports will not have all the components discussed in the following slides.

---

### Prefatory Parts 

- The prefatory parts serve as an introduction, giving the reader some sense of what the report is about and what it includes.
$>$ Cover Page and or Title Page
- Almost all formal reports have a Cover or Title Page, perhaps both. These two pages are used in nearly identical ways, yet some report types or organizations require both with a slight modification to the page's purpose.
$>$ A cover page is a very simple, precise, brief way to introduce your report to the reader The cover includes such information as the title of the report, the author's name, the date and a relevant picture. The most important information on the cover is, naturally, the title. Make sure the title is clear, telling the reader exactly what the report is about.
- The cover page is the first thing that is seen: it will be the foundation for first impressions, for better or worse.
- One easy way to make the report stand out is to use a theme for the report that your audience can connect to.

<u>The Title Fly: </u>The cover is sometimes followed by the title fly, a separate page with only the title on it. The title fly is usually optional, but it does give the report an additional bit of formality.

<u>The title page</u> is an opportunity to provide more specific, detailed information about the document and its authors to its intended audience. It will be very similar to your front cover and it repeats the information on the cover, but adds more important details.
- The title page includes a) the report’s title, b) the name, title, and affiliation of the person or group who authorized the report, c) the name, title, and affiliation of the person or group that prepared the report, and d) the submission date. If the report is relatively short, the title page serves in place of the cover.
<span style="color:rgb(0, 176, 80)">The letter of authorization</span>- a formal letter or memo requesting that the report be prepared is sometimes included, as well as the letter of acceptance –a letter or memo in which the writer of the report acknowledges the request.



---

<u>Transmittal Letter</u> 
- A transmittal letter is sent to the company or business leader who requested the report. This letter may be sent separately from the report. This letter can be printed (especially in situations where the report itself is a paper copy), or it can be sent as an email.
- If applicable, the first paragraph should identify who authorized the report and why the report is significant. Provide the purpose of the report in the first paragraph as well. The next paragraph should briefly identify, categorize, and describe the primary and secondary research of the report. Use the concluding paragraph to offer to discuss the report; it is also customary to conclude by thanking the reader for their time and consideration.
- The letter of transmittal should be formatted as a business letter. Some report writers prefer to send a memo of transmittal instead.
- It is most frequently used with reports created by one company and submitted to another, such as those associated with a sales situation. This letter can be used in both informational and analytical reports.
- This letter should be formatted as a standard business letter. It is frequently signed by an officer of the sending company to emphasize the formality of the document and potentially establish legal formality. Pay careful attention to company policy and legal advice. It's also important to note that some companies prefer this same information in another format within the report.

---

<u>An acknowledgment</u> section in a report is typically included near the beginning, after the title page and before the table of contents or the main body of the report. The acknowledgment section is where you express gratitude and recognition for individuals, organizations, or resources that contributed to the completion of the report but may not have been directly involved in the content creation.

<u>The table of contents:</u> The table of contents page features the headings and secondary headings of the report and their page numbers, enabling audience members to quickly locate specific parts of the report. Leaders (i.e. spaced or unspaced dots) are used to guide the reader's eye from the headings to their page numbers. The table of contents is written after the rest of the report has been typed so you can indicate the page numbers of the various sections and headings. All of the prefatory parts and supplementary parts are included in the table of contents.

<u>Tables of Exhibits or Illustrations: </u>There may be a few different situations in which you should use additional tables of exhibits or illustrations; for example, these tables may be useful to include if your figures or tables are referred to repeatedly throughout your text. Additionally, as a rule of thumb, you should include a table of exhibits when your report is approximately 15 pages or more. This also allows your readers to flip between exhibits more easily in order to compare them.

---

<u>An executive summary</u> is just as the name says: it summarizes all the materials that follow in the report. This section is different from an introduction as it summarizes the entire report, rather than simply introducing it or laying out the structure for the reader. A good way to approach the executive summary is to write it as if the executive or decision maker will only read this section, even though that's unlikely to be the case. This section is found in longer reports and is less likely to be found in a shorter report. It can also be used in both informational and analytical reports.
- Executive summaries should be written after the entire report is completed.

### Text Part 

<u>Introduction:</u>
- The body of a formal report begins with an introduction.
- Most report introductions address the following elements: background information, problem or purpose, significance, scope, methods, organization, and sources. As you may have noticed, some parts of a formal report fulfill similar purposes. Information from the letter of transmittal and the executive summary may be repeated in the introduction. Reword the information in order to avoid sounding repetitive.

<u>The body </u>includes the detailed information that will support your main idea, conclusions and recommendations. ### Supplementary Parts 

- The supplementary parts are all the detailed information placed at the back of the report.
- Appendices (or appendix): Appendices are used to provide readers with additional details that are relevant to the main text but may be too extensive or distracting if included within the body of the document. Among the information included in the appendix of business reports are sample questionnaires, sample forms, computer printouts, statistical formulas, financial statements and spreadsheets, copiers of important documents, and complex illustrations.
  Appendices are usually labeled with letters (e.g., Appendix A, Appendix B) and are attached at the end of the document.
- A glossary is a list of specialized terms or jargon used in the document, along with their definitions or explanations. It helps readers understand the meaning of technical or unfamiliar terms. Glossary entries are typically arranged alphabetically, and each term is followed by its definition.
- An index is a detailed alphabetical list of topics, terms, or names along with the page numbers where they can be found in the document. It serves as a quick reference guide for readers to locate specific information within the document.
Works Cited
- All formal reports should include a works cited page; his page documents the sources cited within the report. The recipient(s) of the report can also refer to this page to locate sources for further research.
- It is acceptable to follow MLA (Modern Language Association), CMS (Chicago Manual of Style), or APA (American Psychological Association) documentation style for entries on this page. Arrange all sources alphabetically.
