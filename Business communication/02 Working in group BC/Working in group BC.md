### Working in Groups and Teams

- A group refers to a collection of individuals who interact with each other, share a common purpose, and are aware of their membership in the group.
- Groups can be formal or informal and may or may not have a designated leader.
- They come together for various reasons, such as social needs, shared interests, or work requirements. Members may not necessarily depend on each other to achieve individual goals.
- A team is a specific type of group that is characterized by a high degree of interdependence among its members. Team members work collaboratively to achieve a common goal or complete a specific task.
- Teams often have a clear structure, roles, and responsibilities. Interdependence is a key feature, as team members rely on each other's contributions to achieve the team's objectives.
- In summary, while all teams are groups, not all groups are teams. Teams represent a specific subset of groups characterized by a higher degree of interdependence and a shared commitment to achieving common objectives.
- Both groups and teams are integral to the functioning of organizations, and they play key roles in achieving common goals.

### The stages of team building 

- The stages of team building, often referred to as the Tuckman's stages of group development, were first introduced by psychologist Bruce Tuckman in 1965. The model outlines the typical phases that teams go through as they work together towards a common goal.
- Researchers and practitioners have build upon existing models or propose variations to better suit specific contexts or contemporary understandings of group development. The stages are

#### 1. Forming

- Characteristics
- Stage of building team- selection of members based on skills and expertise
- Members are assigned with specific responsibilities and authorities
- Dependence on the leader for guidance.
- Introduction, ice-breaking, and orientation activities.

---

#### 2. Storming 

- Bring together the member and mutually discuss about responsibilities
- Open communication, resolving conflicts, and establishing norms.
- Differences in working styles, ideas, and perspectives become apparent.
- Conflict and competition arise as team members express their opinions and vie for positions.


#### 3. Norming

- Socialize members with the view to develop intimacy
- Resolving conflicts and developing norms and roles.
- Greater cohesion and a sense of team identity.
- Collaboration and a focus on team goals.
- Team activity of establishing team norms, building relationships, and defining team processes.

---

#### 4. Performing 

- This stage is concerned with the value and output contributed by member of team.
- Assessment of team and its members to evaluate performance
- levels of trust, motivation, and collaboration.
- Team members are competent, autonomous, and able to handle decisionmaking.
- Focus on achieving the team's goals.
- Develop sincerity to job and high sense of accountability


#### 5. Adjourning

- Task Completion
- Good feeling about Achievement
- Recognition

It's important to note that teams n't always progress linearly through these stages, and they may revisit stages as circumstances change.

### Group interactions 

- Group interactions within an organization refer to the ways in which individuals within a group or team communicate, collaborate, and work together to achieve common goals. These interactions can occur at various levels and in different contexts within the organization.
- Effective group interactions contribute to a positive organizational culture, increased productivity, and the achievement of shared objectives.
- Two heads are better than one head.
- The term 'group dynamics' means the study of forces within a group. Since human beings have an innate desire for belonging to a group, group dynamism is bound to occur.
- The term "dynamics" is derived from the Greek word "dunamis," which means "force" or "power. It refers to the forces and interactions that shape the functioning and development of a group or organization
- The social process by which people interact with one another in small groups can be called group dynamism. It refers to the patterns of interactions and relationships that develop within a group of individuals.

### Characteristics of a group 

1. Specific group goals Groups typically have shared objectives, purposes, or tasks that unify members and guide their collective efforts.
2. Group structure Groups often have a structure that defines roles, responsibilities, and relationships among members. This structure helps in organizing and coordinating group activities.
3. Group pattern recurring and observable behaviors, interactions, or trends within a group.
4. Group norms and values Groups develop norms, which are shared rules or expectations that guide the behavior of members. Norms help establish acceptable and unacceptable conduct within the group.
5. Group size Groups can vary in size, and the number of members can influence the dynamics of the group. Larger groups may have more diversity but might face challenges in coordination, while smaller groups may be more cohesive.
6. Duration Groups can exist for a short or extended period. The duration of a group may impact its goals, norms, and overall dynamics.
7. InfluenceGroup members influence each other through opinions, ideas, and behaviors. Influence can come from various sources, including leaders, experts, or peers within the group.

### Determining group goals 

- Understand Organizational Objectives
	- Start by understanding the broader goals and objectives of the organization. This provides context for aligning the group's goals with the overall mission.
- Conduct a Needs Assessment
	- Assess the needs and challenges within the specific department or team. Identify areas that require improvement, development, or strategic focus.
- Involve Team Members
	- Foster a collaborative approach by involving team members in the goal-setting process. Gather input, insights, and suggestions to ensure a sense of ownership.
- Prioritize Goals
	- Prioritize goals based on their relevance, importance, and alignment with both the organizational and team objectives. Consider short-term and long-term goals.
- SMART Criteria
	- Ensure that goals are Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Timebound (SMART). This makes them clear, quantifiable, attainable, relevant to the team's mission, and time-specific.
 - Align with Organizational Values 
	- Ensure that the group goals align with the values and principles of the organization. This promotes consistency and integration with the broader organizational culture.
- Consider Individual Skills and Roles
	- Take into account the skills, strengths, and roles of individual team members when setting goals. This ensures that goals are realistic and achievable based on the team's composition.
- Create a Roadmap
	- Develop a roadmap or action plan that outlines the steps required to achieve each goal. This provides a clear path for implementation and progress tracking.
- Establish Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)
	- Define specific Key Performance Indicators that can be used to measure progress and success. This adds a quantitative dimension to goal assessment.
- Communication of Goals
	- Clearly communicate the established goals to all team members. Transparency and understanding are key to fostering commitment and accountability.
- Encourage Collaboration
	- Foster a collaborative environment where team members support each other in achieving shared goals. Encourage open communication and idea-sharing.
- Regular Review and Adaptation
	- Regularly review progress towards goals and be open to adapting them based on changing circumstances or organizational priorities.

- Feedback Mechanisms
	- Establish feedback mechanisms to gather insights from team members on the effectiveness of the goals and the strategies employed to achieve them.
- Celebrate Achievements
	- Celebrate milestones and achievements as a team. Recognize and reward collective efforts to boost morale and motivation.
- Reassess and Set New Goals
	- Once a set of goals is achieved, reassess the team's needs, the organizational context, and set new goals for continuous improvement and development.

### Differences between a group, a crowd, and a mob

**Group :** <u>A group is a collection of individuals who interact with each other, share common goals or interests, and have a sense of identity.</u> Groups often have a formal or informal structure with roles, norms, and communication patterns. Members join a group for various reasons, such as socializing, working on common goals, or sharing similar interests. Groups are generally stable over time and can exist for extended periods.
**Examples** Work teams, social clubs, study groups, families.

**A crowd** <u>is a larger gathering of people in a specific location, often characterized by a shared focus or interest, but without the same level of organization as a group.</u> Crowds usually form spontaneously and disband after a short duration. Crowds usually form spontaneously and disband after a short duration. Examples Spectators at a sports event, audience at a concert, people gathered for a protest.

**Mob** <u> A mob is a type of crowd characterized by intense emotion, often involving aggressive or violent behavior. Mobs may form for various reasons, including protest, rioting, or expressing collective anger.
</u>
- Characteristics
	- Unruly Behavior Mobs may exhibit disorderly and often destructive behavior, driven by a shared emotional intensity.
	- Lack of Rationality Individual decision-making within a mob is often influenced by the collective emotions, leading to impulsive actions.
	- Potential for Violence Mobs can pose a risk of violence or harm to people or property.
	- **Examples** Rioting crowds, protest mobs, mobs forming during emergencies.

>**Group** Structured, stable, with organized interactions and shared goals.
>**Crowd** Larger gathering, temporary, with a shared focus but less organization than a group.
>**Mob** A specific type of crowd characterized by intense emotion, unruly behavior, and a potential for violence.

### Advantages of working in groups 

- Diverse Perspectives: Groups bring together individuals with different backgrounds, experiences, and skills.
- Increased Creativity: Collaborative environments often stimulate creativity.
- Efficient Division of Labor: Groups can divide tasks based on individual strengths and expertise, leading to a more efficient distribution of work.
- Skill Development: Working in a group provides opportunities for individuals to develop and enhance a variety of skills, including communication, teamwork, leadership, and conflict resolution.
- Enhanced Learning: Group members can share their knowledge and expertise, leading to a more comprehensive understanding of the subject matter. Learning from others can accelerate individual learning and contribute to a deeper understanding of the material.
- Increased Motivation and Accountability: The presence of peers can motivate individuals to perform at their best. Group members may feel a sense of accountability to the team, leading to higher levels of commitment and effort.
- Pooling of Resources: Groups provide access to a collective pool of resources, including knowledge, skills, and materials. This shared resource pool can lead to more effective problem-solving and decision-making.
- Social Support: Working in a group can provide emotional and social support.
- Faster Problem-Solving: In a group, issues and challenges can be addressed more quickly as multiple minds collaborate to find solutions.
- Improved Decision-Making: Group decision-making often involves a collective evaluation of options and perspectives.
- Enhanced Communication Skills: Group members have the opportunity to express their ideas clearly, listen actively, and provide constructive feedback, contributing to the development of strong communication skills.
- Sense of Belonging: Group work can create a sense of belonging. This feeling of being part of a team can boost morale and job satisfaction

---

### Drawbacks of Working in a Group/ Team 

- Coordination Challenges: Group work requires effective coordination among team members. If there is a lack of proper communication or organization, it can lead to confusion and inefficiency.
- Conflict and Disagreements: Differences in opinions, work styles, or personalities can lead to conflicts within the group. Managing and resolving these conflicts can be time-consuming and may impact the overall productivity.
- Free-Riding or Social Loafing: In some group situations, there may be individuals who contribute less effort, relying on others to the work. This phenomenon is known as free-riding or social loafing and can undermine the success of the group.
- Unequal Participation: Group members may not contribute equally to the project, leading to an uneven distribution of workload. This can result in frustration among team members who feel that they are carrying a disproportionate burden.
- Decision-Making Challenges: Group decision-making can be a lengthy process, especially if there are disagreements or if consensus is required.
- Sacrifice of individual personal perception, identity and dignity.
- Social Dynamics: Social dynamics within a group can impact productivity. Cliques, favoritism, or exclusionary behavior may create a negative atmosphere and hinder collaboration.
- Dependency on Others: In a group, individuals may become overly dependent on their teammates, which can hinder personal growth and problem-solving skills. Some may rely on others to take the lead, leading to a lack of individual initiative.
- Scheduling Challenges: Coordinating schedules for group meetings and collaborative work can be challenging, especially when team members have conflicting commitments or work at different locations.
- Diversity Challenges: While diversity within a group can bring different perspectives and ideas, it can also lead to misunderstandings or difficulties in finding common ground.
- Loss of Individual Accountability: In a group setting, individual accountability may decrease as team members may assume that others will take care of certain tasks. This can result in a diffusion of responsibility.

### Giving and Receiving Feedback in Group 

- Effective feedback is crucial for fostering a positive and productive work environment within groups in an organization.

#### Giving Feedback in Groups

- Be Specific and Constructive Clearly articulate the specific behaviors or actions you are addressing. Focus on constructive feedback that helps individuals or the group improve.
- Use the "I" Statements Express your feedback using "I" statements to take ownership of your perspective and avoid sounding accusatory. For example, say "I noticed" instead of "You always."
- Balance Positive and Negative Feedback Recognize both strengths and areas for improvement. Balancing positive feedback with constructive criticism helps maintain motivation and morale.
- Timeliness Matters Provide feedback in a timely manner. Waiting too long can diminish the impact and relevance of the feedback.
- Choose the Right Setting Consider the context and choose an appropriate setting for giving feedback. Sometimes, private discussions may be more suitable, while other situations may warrant group feedback.
- Encourage Dialogue Foster an open and honest dialogue. Encourage team members to ask questions and seek clarification to ensure a mutual understanding of the feedback.
- Be Solution-Oriented Instead of solely pointing out problems, offer suggestions or solutions. Collaboratively discuss how improvements can be made.
- Use Descriptive Language Use descriptive language to paint a clear picture of the observed behavior. Avoid making assumptions about intentions or motivations.

#### Receiving Feedback in Groups 

- Be Open-Minded Approach feedback with an open mind. Resist the urge to be defensive, and instead, focus on understanding the perspective being shared.
- Listen Actively Actively listen to the feedback. Give the speaker your full attention and avoid interrupting. Seek clarification if needed to ensure a complete understanding.
- Ask Clarifying Questions If the feedback is unclear, ask for specific examples or details.
- Avoid a Defensive Response Even if the feedback is challenging, resist becoming defensive. Acknowledge the feedback, and express gratitude for the insights. Remember, feedback is an opportunity for growth.
- Reflect and ConsiderTake some time to reflect on the feedback. Consider how it aligns with your own observations and identify actionable steps for improvement.
- Express AppreciationThank the person providing the feedback for their honesty and willingness to communicate. A positive and appreciative response encourages continued open communication.
- Follow Up If applicable, follow up with the person who provided the feedback to update them on your progress and any changes you've implemented.
- Continuous Improvement Mindset: Embrace a continuous improvement mindset. See feedback as a valuable tool for personal and professional development.

---

### Characteristics of feedback 

- Specificity: Good feedback is specific and detailed. It provides clear examples and details about the observed behavior or performance.
- Timeliness: Timely feedback is more effective as it is given close to the time of the observed behavior or performance.
- Constructiveness: Constructive feedback focuses on improvement rather than criticism
- Clarity: Feedback should be clear and easily understood.
- Relevance \& Goal-Oriented: Feedback should be relevant to the goals and objectives at hand. Feedback should be tied to specific goals and objectives.
- Respectfulness: Constructive feedback is delivered respectfully, focusing on the behavior or performance rather than making personal judgments.
- Two-way Communication: Effective feedback allows for a two-way communication process.
- Future-Oriented: Effective feedback not only addresses current performance but also looks toward the future.
- Credibility: Feedback is more impactful when it comes from a credible source. The person providing feedback should have the knowledge and experience relevant to the subject.

### Popular Schools of Thought on Conflict

#### 1. Structuralist school of thought- 18^{th} century: 
 
Structuralists analyze conflicts as an outcome of structural deficiency in the organization. Organizations have structural divisions that result in divisions of groups into rulers and those who are ruled. They seek to understand how these structures contribute to conflicts. #### 2. Marxist school of thought - $19^{\text {th }}$ century: 

The Marxist school of thought on conflict is grounded in the ideas of Karl Marx, a 19th-century German philosopher, economist, and political theorist. In an organizational context, the Marxist school of thought on conflict focuses on the idea that conflicts arise from the unequal distribution of power and resources within the workplace.

- **In organizations, there are usually two main groups: the people who own or control the company (bosses or management) and the people who work for them (employees)**. Marxists argue that **conflicts emerge because these groups have different interests**, with management seeking to maximize profits, while employees want better working conditions and fair treatment.
- The profit made by the organization is seen as a result of the labor of workers, and conflicts arise when workers feel they are not fairly compensated.
- Conflict is also seen in terms of power dynamics. Management holds power and makes decisions, while workers often have limited say in how things are run.
- According to the Marxist perspective, organizations function to serve the interests of the owning or controlling class (management or capitalists). The organizational structure and policies are seen as tools to maintain the existing social order and reinforce the dominance of the management.
- Marxists suggest that conflicts within organizations could lead to change if workers become aware of their collective interests and challenge the existing power structures.

#### Psycho analytical school of thought – 2Oth century

The psychoanalytic school of thought on conflict, founded by Sigmund Freud, focuses on how unconscious desires and emotions create inner tensions.

➢ Sigmund Freud proposed the existence of two fundamental and opposing drives or instincts that shape human behavior. These are often referred to as the life instinct (Eros) and the death instinct (Thanatos).

➢ Freud proposed that our psyche is divided into three parts — the id (instinctual desires), the ego (reality-driven), and the superego (moral conscience). Conflict often arises between these three aspects of our psyche.

- Eros (Life Instinct):
	------Eros is the life instinct and is associated with the drive for self-preservation and the preservation of the species. It encompasses all the constructive and life-affirming tendencies, including the desire for love, creativity, cooperation, and the pursuit of pleasure. Eros is the force that binds individuals together and promotes life-sustaining activities.

- Thanatos (Death Instinct):
	 - Thanatos, on the other hand, is the death instinct and represents the drive toward aggression, destruction. Freud theorized that there is an inherent human tendency towards self-destruction and aggression. While Eros promotes life and unity, Thanatos represents the destructive and aggressive tendencies that can lead to conflict and harm.
	 - According to Freud, the interplay and conflict between these two instincts contribute to the complexity of human behavior.

Ideas from psychoanalysis that have been considered in the context of organizations:

- Individuals may not always be fully aware of their motivations and behaviors. Unconscious thoughts, desires, and conflicts can influence decision-making, interpersonal relationships, and overall behavior in the workplace.
- Individuals often employ defense mechanisms to cope with anxiety and stress. For example, denial of problems or displacement of frustration onto colleagues.
- The relationships between leaders and subordinates may be analyzed in terms of power, influence, and unconscious dynamics.

### Techniques of conflict management 

- Communication: Open and honest communication is fundamental to conflict resolution.
- Negotiation: Negotiation involves a process where conflicting parties discuss and compromise to reach a mutually agreeable solution.
- Meditation: Mediation involves a neutral third party (the mediator) who facilitates communication between conflicting parties. The mediator helps them explore issues, identify common ground, and work towards a resolution.
- Arbitration: Arbitration is a form of alternative dispute resolution (ADR) commonly used in conflict management. It involves the intervention of a neutral third party, known as an arbitrator, who is appointed to make a binding decision on the dispute. Arbitration provides a more formalized process than negotiation or mediation and is often utilized in legal, business, and contractual disputes.
- Reconciliation: Reconciliation focuses on healing the emotional wounds caused by conflict and restoring a sense of trust and understanding between the parties involved. It acknowledges the human aspect of conflicts and seeks to mend the emotional and psychological impact.
- Problem-Solving: Applying a problem-solving approach involves identifying the root causes of the conflict and working together to find practical solutions. It emphasizes addressing underlying issues rather than focusing solely on symptoms.
- Assertion: Assertiveness involves expressing one's needs, opinions, and concerns in a clear and respectful manner. Encouraging individuals to assert themselves can help prevent conflicts from escalating.
